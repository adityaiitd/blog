Simulation-backed preliminary design. Not production-qualified. Hazardous high voltage. Independent safety and regulatory review required before any hardware is built.

What is this thing, in plain terms

The problem

An AI rack now draws hundreds of kilowatts. Delivering that at 12 volts would need absurdly thick copper, so power is distributed at 800 volts and stepped down right next to the chips. Something has to do that 64:1 conversion efficiently, in a space about 8 mm tall.

The trick

Doing 800 V→12.5 V in one jump is hard, so the converter is split into eight identical cells stacked in series. Each cell sees only one eighth of the voltage — about 100 V — and each supplies one eighth of the current. Their outputs are wired in parallel. That turns one brutal 64:1 problem into eight manageable 8:1 problems.

What you are designing

The transformer inside one of those cells. It is not a wound component: the windings are copper layers etched into a printed circuit board, sandwiched around a ferrite core. That is what "planar" means.

The four things that fight each other

Turns versus flux
More turns means less magnetic flux in the core, so less core loss — but more copper, more resistance, and more layers.
Interleaving versus capacitance
Stacking primary and secondary layers alternately cuts resistive loss sharply. It also puts them closer together more often, which builds a capacitor that injects noise into the output.
Magnetising inductance versus switching
A small magnetising inductance lets the circuit switch losslessly, but wastes current going round in circles even at no load.
Frequency versus everything
Higher frequency shrinks the magnetics but makes copper loss worse, because current stops using the middle of the conductor.

There is no setting where all four are happy. The sliders let you find out which one is actually stopping you.
